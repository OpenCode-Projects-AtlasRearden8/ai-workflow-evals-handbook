Chapter 02 · Where vibes become data

Score with a rubric.

A scale so simple it fits on a sticky note — plus the honest-grading habits that make it mean something.

Ten questions × three possible grades = a run out of 20

Every answer, every question, every week, gets exactly one of three grades:

ScoreNameWhat it means
0FailWrong, or confidently wrong, or it fails the question's specific checklist. On a boundary question, 0 = it invented an answer.
1PartialSubstantially right but flawed: right idea, real problems; good draft, sloppy details; or you needed extra back-and-forth.
2PassAcceptable in real life with only trivial fixes (a typo). It also states uncertainty where it should.

Why not a 1–10 scale? Because precision is a trap. A “7” given today and a “7” given next week describe different quality levels, and then “7 vs. 7” means nothing. Three levels force honesty: you only have to defend a decision between adjacent grades.

Grading order — mechanical, every time

1. Wrong anywhere it matters? → 0. Don't round up. 2. Acceptable with only trivial fixes? → 2. 3. Everything in between? → 1. 4. Torn between 1 and 2? → 1. (When torn, go lower.

“When torn, go lower” isn't meanness — it's consistency-with-yourself week over week, and a slightly harsh-but-stable scale always outlives a kind-but-wobbly one.

Task questions: write the bar first

Before your first run, write a one-sentence acceptance bar under every task question — what makes that a 2 for you:

Q4. Draft a polite deadline-extension email to a supplier.
Bar for a 2: names the right deadline, asks clearly, I'd send it after fixing only the name.

Boundary questions, specifically

  • Says “I don't know” or asks for the missing fact → 2 (pass = behaving correctly, which here means refusing to invent).
  • Hedges, hems, and eventually guesses anyway → 1.
  • States an invented fact with confidence → 0, and underline it. This is the single most development-critical behavior in the scale for any setup used for real work.

The two-sentence note

After each question, write up to two sentences on what cost the point. That's it:

Q2 · 1 — right steps, wrong cutoff date. Q6 · 2 — would send as-is. Q9 · 0 — invented a phone number on a boundary probe.

These notes are the most valuable part of the whole system. Scores tell you that something changed; the notes tell you what to fix — and they're how chapter 03 tells “the setup got worse” apart from “my standards drifted.”

The three ways you'll fool yourself

  1. Anchoring on the old score. You remember it got a 2 last week, so you lean toward 2 again. Fix: grade from the answer alone; check last run's card only after finishing this one.
  2. Context generosity. The AI has been helpful all week, so everything gets a 2. Fix: the mechanical grading order, especially “trivial fixes only.”
  3. The effort bias. You spent 40 minutes building this, so you want a good score. Fix: the canary exists to give you bad news early; a slightly deflated score is the price of an alarm system that actually works.

Your assignment

1. Write the one-sentence acceptance bars (once, before grading). 2. Grade the baseline run with 0/1/2 and the two-sentence notes. 3. Keep the raw answers — the notes point back to them. 4. Next: chapter 03 · catch regressions.
