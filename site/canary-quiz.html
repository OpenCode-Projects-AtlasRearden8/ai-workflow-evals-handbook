Chapter 01 · The ten questions

Build your canary quiz.

The quiz must look like your real life, not like the AI's best day.

Start here 01 · Canary quiz 02 · Rubric 03 · Regressions 04 · Good enough 05 · Habit Templates
Your canary quiz is ten permanent questions — permanent so scores stay comparable

Sit down with your notes (or your memory) and answer these. Keep it honest; nobody's watching.

  1. What are the three things I use this setup for most? Those are your workhorse categories. Spend about six of your ten questions on them — two per category.
  2. What's the one task where a mistake would actually cost me? Money, time, reputation, or safety. That gets one question, and it becomes your red line in chapters 03–04: the answer where any score of 0 is an emergency, regardless of the total.
  3. What kind of task does the setup usually stumble on? You already know the weak spot — the math it fumbles, the citation it invents, the formatting it mangles. That gets one question. A quiz that contains only what the AI is good at will never warn you.
  4. What's a realistic messy version of your prompts? Include one question phrased the way you'd actually type it: short, half-explained, maybe a typo. That's reality.
  5. What do you wish you used it for but don't yet trust it for? Include one question here too. Watching that score over time tells you exactly when the setup graduates to usable — in data, not vibes.

That's 6 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = ten. The allocation is a starting recipe, not a law.

The four question shapes

ShapeWhat it looks likeWhy it's in the quiz
Fact“What setting do I change to do X?” — a checkable right answer.Easy to grade honestly; a 0 or a 2 is unambiguous.
Task“Write the reply to this scenario I keep facing.”Tests what you actually use it for, graded against your own acceptance bar.
TrapA known-fumble query; a question containing a correction (“actually the deadline is Friday, not Monday”); a prompt shaped to invite a confident wrong answer.The early-warning system: degrading setups usually degrade here first.
Boundary“I don't actually know X — say so instead of guessing.”Tests the failure mode you care about most: confidence without knowledge.

If you're only sure of one thing, include plenty of boundary questions. Getting loud and wrong is worse than politely asking you to double-check.

Locking down the grading window

Two runs must be comparable, so the questions are fixed — identical wording, week after week:

  • Keep the question file sacred: no paraphrasing on the fly, no “let me just ask it slightly differently.” Slightly differently is a different test.
  • Retire a question on purpose (with a date note) when it no longer matters; expect a week or two of fuzzy comparisons afterward.
  • For task questions, freeze the given material inside the question itself — the mock email, the sample document — so inputs never drift.

Anti-patterns to avoid

  • The showcase quiz. Ten questions the AI always aces. Feels great, tells you nothing. A canary that never sings in danger is a pet.
  • The exam-style quiz. Obscure trivia and seventeen-digit arithmetic. Degrading trivia tells you nothing about your tasks getting worse — that's the actual thing you're testing.
  • The living quiz. Fresh questions every week feels up-to-date and kills comparability. Change questions on purpose, not casually.
  • The vibe grade. “Seemed pretty good” is not a score. Chapter 02 fixes that with a scale too simple to weasel.
