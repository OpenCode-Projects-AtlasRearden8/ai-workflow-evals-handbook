Chapter 03 · The comparison ritual

Catch regressions.

A regression is when a change makes something worse without you noticing. Here's the mechanical way to catch one.

Run the comparison when anything changes — model, prompt, workflow, or nothing at all for a quarter

Regenergressions are sneaky because they usually arrive bundled with improvements: the overall vibe stays positive while one important ability quietly dies. This is a short, four-step procedure — you need two filled scorecards, OLD and NEW.

The four steps

  1. Compare totals. NEW − OLD: up or equal → keep going (still do step 3); down 1–3 → real signal, take step 2 seriously; down 4+ → strong signal, stop until understood.
  2. Question-by-question. Same → skip. Better → note it. Worse by one → worth a note. Worse by two (2→0) → step 3, even if the total went up.
  3. Red-line check. If your red-line question or any boundary probe dropped to 0, that's an emergency regardless of the total. Then read every note under a dropped score — one sentence usually shows exactly what broke.
  4. Write the one-line verdict in the margin of the new scorecard. That log entry is what future-you will otherwise be guessing about.

The verdict line, by example

NEW vs OLD: 15/20 → 17/20 · Q7 dropped 2→1 (rambles; loses the actual question) · red-line OK · green-lined, ship it.

The four patterns worth memorizing

PatternTotals sayWhat it meansVerdict
Clean winUp 1–5, no red-line issuesThe change worked, or it's a harmless model shiftShip it
Net-zero bleedSame total, questions tradedCapability shifting under you; watch the trend 2–3 runsShip with notes
The cliffTotal crashes 4+Something material brokeStop; diagnose before use
The leakUp or flat, red-line 0 or a 2→0An improvement in one place, a sold-out ability elsewhereDo not ship the affected task; fix or fence it off

The leak is the quiet way real setups drift into trouble — it's why the red-line check is a numbered step, not a paragraph.

What to do when you find one

  1. Diff your own changes first. Most regressions come from you — which is great, because those are fixable.
  2. Roll back and re-run the quiz. Score returns → change isolated; usually one sentence of instruction repairs it.
  3. Check for a silent model update. You can't undo it, but you can compensate — or consciously accept the tradeoff.
  4. Nothing explains it? Re-run once, a day later. Flaky runs exist; one flaky run isn't a verdict, two in a row is.

When the model didn't drift — your grading did

Sometimes scores drop… but the notes read like your standards tightened (“right idea, wanted more structure”). That's grading drift. Fixes: rewrite the acceptance bars to be concrete, re-grade both cards back-to-back in one sitting, and keep notes short — the two-sentence rule actively prevents drift.

Your assignment

1. Fill one OLD scorecard now, even if nothing changed. Baselines come before surprises. 2. At your next real change: rerun, compare, handwrite the one-line verdict. Next: chapter 04 · when good is good enough.
